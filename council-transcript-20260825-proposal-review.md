# LLM Council Transcript: Senior by Design Proposal Review

**Date:** 2026-08-25
**Question source:** User request to council-review the SbD proposal deck on three fronts (UX/slide count, polish/competitiveness, engagement models)

---

## Framed Question

A solo/small consultancy is pitching a services proposal to Senior By Design (SbD), a boutique senior-living interior-design firm: ~22 staff, 12 years in business, ~$7M breakeven revenue, $19M in signed work for next year (new builds only), 8-9 active clients, zero advertising/zero sales team (100% referral-driven growth), and — for the first time ever — turning down new work because operations can't stretch further.

SbD's current tools: Access (custom-built quoting/inventory/finish-schedule database), Smartsheet (project management), QuickBooks (accounting, gatekept exclusively by an employee named Jimmy), Monograph (just adopted, time-tracking/design fees), Trainual (SOPs), Microsoft Teams/SharePoint, ChatGPT (email drafting), an unused Monday.com board (built to share with one difficult client, "ESL," who never logged in — now paying for 10 unused licenses), and an AI phone bot for warehouse calls.

Core pain points uncovered in discovery: (1) duplicate manual data entry across Access and Smartsheet for every request; (2) no automation between stage changes and downstream actions; (3) zero visibility into QuickBooks for anyone but Jimmy; (4) inconsistent project naming across systems; (5) ~60 one-off/remodel requests with no formal timeline structure; (6) designers go silent for days/weeks; (7) no documented approval trail for design decisions — disputed months later, worst with client "ESL."

Proposed solution: a 5-phase automation engagement (Phase 1: $45K/90-day quote-to-cash sync; Phases 2-5: $120-180K/12mo covering decision documentation, team enablement, resource planning, culture integration). Current deck: 21 slides. Currently one commercial structure: fixed-fee, phased.

**The council was asked for recommendations on:**
1. UX/UI — cut the deck to ≤15 slides while still landing problem → solution → why → so-what → next-steps.
2. Polish/competitiveness — make the proposal read as more professional and competitive.
3. Commercial structure — add two engagement-model options: a T&M contract, and a fractional W2 hire (12-18 months).

---

## Advisor Responses

### The Contrarian
The fractional W2 offer is the thing that can actually ruin you. Kill it. On payroll you get: IP assignment (their employee handbook will make every automation you build work-for-hire — you lose the reusable asset that lets you sell this same engagement to the next design firm), at-will termination with no notice or kill fee, non-solicit/non-compete exposure in the exact vertical you're trying to build a practice in, and Glenn's team treating you as headcount to reassign. You also hand SbD a payroll-tax and benefits headache administered by Jimmy — the person whose gatekeeping you're proposing to dismantle. Offer fractional-CTO-as-1099-retainer if you want recurring revenue. Do not sign a W-2 with a client under operational strain who might need to cut heads.

Second flaw: your headline ROI doesn't clear your price. 6-8 hrs/week down to 1 hr/week is roughly $15-20K/year of recovered admin time against a $45K fee. Any competent buyer runs that math and sees a 2.5-year payback. Meanwhile you're sitting on the actual six-figure number and burying it in Phase 2: they are turning down work against $19M signed, and ESL is denying approvals they can't disprove. Lead with turned-down revenue and litigation-grade approval trails. Deposit reconciliation is a footnote, not a hero metric.

Third: you fixed-bid an undocumented custom Access database. Who built it? Is there an API, or are you writing ODBC glue against a schema nobody has documented? Fixed fee on legacy unknowns means you eat 100% of discovery overrun.

Fourth: Jimmy isn't a detail, he's the single point of failure. Phase 1 dies without QuickBooks credentials from the one person whose entire leverage is exclusive access. No slide addresses him.

Fifth: they bought Monday.com and never used it. That's the reference class for this deal. Your risk slide should say so.

### The First Principles Thinker
You're optimizing a deck when the underlying deal isn't defined yet. Strip it down. SbD has $19M signed, no sales function, and is turning down work. They do not have a software integration problem. They have a throughput and liability problem, and every number in your deck measures the wrong thing.

"Deposit reconciliation drops from 6-8 hrs/week to 1 hr" is roughly $15K/year of a bookkeeper's time. You're asking $45K. That math only works if Glenn is buying on faith. The real number is sitting in pain point #7. A client denies approving a ceiling detail; someone eats the rework. Ask Glenn, before you send anything: what did the last three disputed change orders cost you, and how many did ESL alone generate? If that's $200K/year, your Phase 2 is Phase 1 and the $45K is a rounding error.

Second reframe: three commercial options is not generosity, it's a confession. Offering fixed-fee, T&M, and fractional W2 tells Glenn you don't know what this engagement is. Two options, maximum, with a recommendation.

Third: the fractional W2 isn't a pricing model, it's a different life. Payroll means his HR policies, his IP assignment, his non-compete, his unemployment exposure — and single-client concentration for you at a firm already at operational breaking point. Don't price it as "option C." Either you believe embedding is the right structure and you lead with it, or it doesn't belong in the deck.

Fifteen slides is achievable trivially once you stop selling five phases and start selling one quantified problem.

### The Expansionist
You are pricing a capacity problem like a bookkeeping problem. SbD is turning down work for the first time in 12 years against $19M signed. The deck's headline metric is "6-8 hrs/week down to 1 hr." That's a $15K story told to a firm whose real question is "how much of the work we're declining can we say yes to?" Reframe Phase 1's success metric as projects absorbed without headcount. One recovered new-build project likely dwarfs the entire $120-180K program. $45K is 0.24% of next year's signed revenue — you are underpriced, and underpricing reads as under-serious against competing vendors.

The fractional W2 is the highest-upside option, and not for the reason you think. Embedded payroll access to a 100% referral-driven firm means access to the referral engine itself — 8-9 clients who are senior-living operators owning portfolios, not single buildings. Your next five deals are inside SbD's client list. Take the W2 at a lower cash rate in exchange for: named case-study and logo rights, a warm-intro clause, and retained IP ownership of anything generalizable.

Phase 2 is a product, not a deliverable. "Decisions captured from call transcripts into a client-visible approval trail" solves the universal ESL problem for every design, architecture, and construction firm alive. Build it at SbD, own the IP, license it back at $0. You are being paid to build your own company's first product.

Deck: collapse four problem slides into one — "the ceiling," with the turned-down-work number as the hero. Add the three-option comparison table. Competitiveness comes from showing you understand their growth math, not from more slides.

### The Outsider
I'm looking at this cold, and here's what a first-time reader hits. "Train switch" is the biggest problem in the deck. It's an insider metaphor from the discovery call. Glenn may remember it; his CFO reading the PDF alone won't. Same with "Quote-to-Cash Foundation" — that's consultant vocabulary for "when a job is approved, the invoice and the PO go out by themselves." Say that instead. Every phase name is currently a category label, not a promise. "Team Enablement," "Culture Integration," "Resource Planning" — I can't tell what any of those produce or why I'd pay for them.

Phases 3-5 are unpriced, unscoped, and undated, but they're 4 of your 21 slides. To an outsider that reads as filler, or worse, as a hint that the real number is much larger than $45K and you're not saying it yet. Cut them to one slide. That alone gets you close to 15.

The one number that matters is missing. You quantify hours saved but never dollars. $19M signed, turning away work — the outsider question is "what does the backlog cost us?" One slide with a defensible number for revenue currently unbookable, next to $45K, ends the pricing conversation.

The approval-trail problem is your strongest slide and it's buried on slide 18. "A client denies approving the carpet, you eat the cost" is instantly legible to anyone. Move it into problem framing.

On the three options: don't present three. An outsider reading three prices assumes you don't know what the job is worth. Lead with one recommendation, list the other two as a comparison row underneath.

### The Executor
The 21-slide deck already reads as a plan. What it doesn't have is a Monday. Cut to 14 by deleting the slides that describe rather than commit. Kill three of four problem-framing slides — Glenn lived the discovery call, he doesn't need his own pain read back four times. One slide: seven pains, ranked, with hours/week attached. Collapse the Phase 1 six-slide block to three: as-is/to-be diagram, 90-day timeline with named owners, price. Merge culture-protection and risk into one "how this doesn't blow up" slide. Phases 2-5 become a single slide — four boxes, one line each, no deep-dive. That's 14 with the commercial slide added.

On the three models, only one has a real first step. T&M with no cap on a firm that's never bought this before is a slide Glenn's CFO uses to kill the deal — if you offer it, cap it and attach a not-to-exceed. Fractional W2 is the one you're least ready to execute: payroll setup, benefits eligibility, PTO, an employee handbook, workers' comp, and the fact that you can't invoice, can't subcontract, and can't walk in 30 days. Do not put it on the deck unless you have already priced your loaded hourly and asked Jimmy — who owns QuickBooks and therefore payroll — whether SbD can even add a part-time W2 without a state registration headache. That's a phone call this week, not a slide.

The real Monday-morning move: Phase 1 is not a 90-day project, it's a two-week paid discovery ($5-8K) that produces the integration spec and the field-mapping doc. Sell that. It's a signature Glenn can give without a board conversation, it de-risks your $45K estimate, and if the Access schema is a mess you find out on day 9, not day 60.

## Peer Review (anonymization key: A=Expansionist, B=Contrarian, C=Executor, D=Outsider, E=First Principles Thinker)

### Reviewed from the Contrarian's lens
**Strongest: B.** It's the only response with falsifiable, deal-killing specifics: fixed bid against an undocumented Access schema, Jimmy as the credential gatekeeper Phase 1 dies without, and the Monday.com purchase as the reference class for this deal. C is the runner-up — the $5-8K paid discovery is the only concrete de-risking move anyone proposed.

**Biggest blind spot: A.** It tells him to take W2 at reduced cash to mine SbD's client list for his next five deals, and to build his product on their payroll and "license it back at $0." That's soliciting your employer's customers and dodging an IP-assignment clause. A dressed up a conflict of interest as upside and never priced the downside.

**All five missed:** every response pivots the hero metric to turned-down revenue without testing whether admin time is the binding constraint. Also unexamined: who maintains these integrations after the engagement, and what proof of prior delivery the deck offers.

### Reviewed from the First Principles lens
**Strongest: B.** It separates the stated problem (deck length) from the real one (the ROI doesn't clear the price, and the scope rests on an undocumented Access schema you fixed-bid). Jimmy-as-single-point-of-failure and the abandoned Monday.com purchase are the two facts that actually predict this deal's outcome. E has the same frame but stops at the reframe; C is the best tactical answer.

**Biggest blind spot: A.** It advises taking the W2 to mine SbD's referral network and build a product on their payroll while retaining IP. In a 100% referral-driven firm, that's the one behavior that ends the relationship if it surfaces.

**All five missed:** the W2 option exists to solve the consultant's income-stability problem, not SbD's — the deck is quietly negotiating two deals. Nobody named the binding constraint the Monday.com failure reveals: SbD has no internal owner to adopt anything. Without a named one, every option fails identically.

### Reviewed from the Expansionist's lens
**Strongest: A.** It's the only response that treats the engagement as an asset-building move rather than a fee. B, C, D, and E all optimize the same deal downward.

**Biggest blind spot: B.** It kills the W2 on risk alone and misses that every hazard it lists is a negotiable clause, not a property of payroll. B forecloses the highest-optionality path to avoid a drafting conversation.

**All five missed:** the valuation play — a $19M firm turning down work, 100% referral-driven, with operations living in two people's heads, is a succession/PE candidate. Nobody proposed pricing against it or asked Glenn about his 5-year horizon.

*(Note: this reviewer's own advisor identity — the Expansionist — is the one it rated strongest. The chairman treats this pick with reduced independent weight; see synthesis.)*

### Reviewed from the Outsider's lens
**Strongest: C.** It's the only one that actually answers all three questions asked — a specific slide-by-slide path to 14, a concrete position on each engagement model, and a Monday-morning move.

**Biggest blind spot: A.** Joining someone's payroll to mine their clients is a conflict of interest that would end the relationship if Glenn read that slide.

**All five missed proof:** not one advisor asked who else is bidding, or noted that nothing in 21 slides establishes this consultant has done this before — no references, no prior client, no case study. Also unraised: key-man risk. Who maintains these automations if a solo operator is unavailable?

### Reviewed from the Executor's lens
**Strongest: C.** It's the only response that produces a Monday action instead of a critique — a signature Glenn can give without a board conversation, that de-risks the $45K fixed bid and fails fast on day 9.

**Biggest blind spot: A.** It tells you to take the W2 *and* retain IP ownership. Those cancel out — standard employee IP assignment makes everything work-for-hire.

**All five missed the arithmetic:** nobody produced a loaded hourly rate, a T&M range, or a W2 salary equivalent — without those numbers you can't build the comparison table anyone recommends. Also unaddressed: is this deck presented live or emailed cold? And no one put an expiration date on the quote.

## Chairman Synthesis

Only the Expansionist-lens reviewer defended it — and that reviewer also rated the Expansionist's own response as strongest, a self-referential pattern the chairman discounted.
